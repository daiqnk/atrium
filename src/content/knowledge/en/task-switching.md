## "I was busy all day, but nothing got done"

Halfway through a report you glance at a message; after replying, you return to the report and the thread of thought is gone. Repeat a few dozen times and the day is over.

## Study 1: switching itself takes time

In 2001, psychologists Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans published a series of experiments (Rubinstein, Meyer, & Evans, 2001). Participants alternated between tasks with different rules — for example, sorting by shape one moment and by number the next.

**Switching produced extra time costs** compared with repeating the same task, and the costs grew with task complexity and unfamiliarity. The researchers attributed this to the brain having to shift goals and reload the relevant rules.

## Study 2: interrupted people work faster — and feel more stressed

In 2008, Gloria Mark's team at UC Irvine ran an experiment (Mark, Gudith, & Klocke, 2008) in which participants handled a set of email tasks, some with frequent interruptions and some without.

Interestingly, interrupted participants **finished faster**, perhaps compensating by speeding up — but they reported **higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort**.

In other words, the cost of interruption may not show up in the output; it shows up in how drained you feel.

## How to try it

1. **Batch it**: handle messages and email at set times, e.g. every 60–90 minutes
2. **One window at a time**: during deep work, close tabs and notifications you don't need
3. **Keep a "parking lot"**: when something else pops into your head, jot it on paper and come back to it later
4. **Use a timer**: work in 25–50-minute focus blocks, then check messages

## Boundaries

If you have had long-standing trouble concentrating that clearly affects school, work or daily life, there may be other causes (sleep, mood, or attention-related conditions). Please talk to a doctor or professional.
