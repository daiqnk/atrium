## The minutes before you go on

Sweaty palms, pounding heart, a knot in the stomach — before an interview, a talk or an exam, almost everyone knows it. The usual reading is: "I'm too nervous. I'm going to blow it."

The same signals can be read differently: **"My body is mobilizing resources to help me handle this."**

## Research: change the reading, change the response

In 2012, Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues ran an experiment (Jamieson, Nock, & Mendes, 2012). Participants completed a standardized social stress task (such as giving a speech to evaluators). Beforehand:

- one group was told that stress arousal is **functional** and can help performance (**reappraisal**)
- the other groups got no such explanation, or were told to ignore their arousal

The reappraisal group showed more adaptive cardiovascular responses — more efficient cardiac output and lower vascular resistance, a pattern closer to "challenge" than "threat" — and less attentional bias toward negative information.

Another study (Crum, Salovey, & Achor, 2013) found that people's general mindset about stress — "stress is debilitating" vs. "stress is enhancing" — is linked to health symptoms and work performance, and that the mindset can be partly shifted with simple interventions such as short videos.

This does not mean more stress is better. Chronic, excessive stress is still bad for health. Reappraisal is aimed at **short, manageable** stressful moments.

## How to try it

1. Notice the body: "My heart is speeding up, my hands are a little shaky."
2. Reframe it: "This is my body revving up to help me focus."
3. Return to the task: "What's my first sentence going to be?"
4. Afterwards, look back: did the nerves help you this time?

## Boundaries

If you often feel uncontrollable tension, have panic attacks, or stress is affecting your sleep and health, please see a doctor or mental health professional. In a crisis, contact local emergency services (in the US, call or text 988).
