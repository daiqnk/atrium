## A familiar scene

Lights off, head on the pillow — and your mind starts replaying tomorrow's meeting, the unanswered messages, the report that's not done. The harder you try to sleep, the more awake you feel.

Researchers wondered: what if you **wrote those things down** before bed?

## The study

In 2018, Michael Scullin's team at Baylor University ran an overnight sleep-lab experiment (Scullin et al., 2018). Fifty-seven healthy young adults spent five minutes writing before bed, randomly assigned to write either:

- a **to-do list** for the next few days, or
- a list of things they had **already completed**

Sleep onset was measured objectively with polysomnography.

## What they found

- The to-do-list group fell asleep **faster**
- Within that group, the **more specific** the list, the faster people fell asleep

One explanation is that "offloading" unfinished tasks to paper means your brain no longer has to keep rehearsing them. Still, this is a single, modest study in healthy young adults, and the authors call for more research.

## How to try it

1. Five minutes before bed, take a sheet of paper (ideally not your phone)
2. Write what you need to do tomorrow and over the next few days — **be specific**: "Send the quote to Mr. Wang before 10 a.m." beats "work stuff"
3. Put it down and tell yourself: "It's on paper. Tomorrow-me will handle it."
4. If something new pops up after lights-out, get up, add it, and lie back down

## When to get professional help

If trouble falling asleep happens several nights a week for three months or more and affects your days, it may be chronic insomnia. The American College of Physicians recommends **cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** as the initial treatment for chronic insomnia in adults (Qaseem et al., 2016). Please see a doctor or sleep specialist rather than relying on tips alone.
