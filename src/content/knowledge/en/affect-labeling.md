## "Right now I feel…"

When we feel bad, the first instinct is often "don't think about it" or "push it down." Psychology research points to something simpler: **put the feeling into a word** — "I'm anxious," "this is hurt," "a bit disappointed."

Researchers call this **affect labeling**.

## What the research found

In 2007, Matthew Lieberman's team at UCLA ran an fMRI study (Lieberman et al., 2007). Participants viewed emotional faces and sometimes chose which of two **emotion words** matched the face (e.g., "angry" or "scared"), and sometimes did other matching tasks.

Labeling the emotion was associated with **lower amygdala activity** and higher activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, and the two were inversely related.

A later review (Torre & Lieberman, 2018) describes affect labeling as **implicit emotion regulation**: you are not trying to feel better, you are only describing — yet describing can take some of the edge off.

To be clear, these are mostly short-term lab effects. Labeling is not a cure and does not replace treatment.

## How to use it

1. When a feeling surges, pause and ask: "What's one word for what I feel right now?"
2. Be **specific**: "annoyed" might really be "irritated at being interrupted," "hurt at being ignored," or "disappointed in myself"
3. Describe, don't judge: "I feel anxious," not "I'm anxious again, I'm hopeless"
4. Write it down or say it quietly out loud

This connects to our article on *emotional granularity*: the more feeling words you can tell apart, the more precise your labels.

## When to get professional help

If intense anxiety or low mood lasts more than two weeks, or affects your sleep, work or relationships, please reach out to a professional. In a crisis, contact local emergency services (in the US, call or text 988).
