## A comparison

When a friend messes something up and comes to you, you'd probably say: "It's okay, everyone makes mistakes. You did your best."

When you mess up, the inner voice may sound more like: "How could you be so stupid? I knew you couldn't do it."

We are often far kinder to others than to ourselves.

## What self-compassion is

In 2003, psychologist Kristin Neff of the University of Texas at Austin proposed a framework for **self-compassion** (Neff, 2003) with three components:

| Component | Meaning | Opposite |
|---|---|---|
| Self-kindness | Meeting your own pain with understanding and warmth | Self-judgment |
| Common humanity | Recognizing that mistakes and suffering are part of being human | Isolation ("only I am this bad") |
| Mindfulness | Holding painful feelings in balanced awareness, neither suppressing nor amplifying them | Over-identification |

Neff stresses that self-compassion is **not** self-pity, self-indulgence or lowering your standards. It means admitting "I didn't do well" without attacking yourself.

## What the research says

A meta-analysis (MacBeth & Gumley, 2012) pooling 20 samples from 14 studies (about 4,000 people) found a large inverse association (r ≈ −0.54): people with higher self-compassion tended to report fewer symptoms such as depression, anxiety and stress.

These are mostly **correlational** findings — they do not prove that raising self-compassion will reduce depression. Think of it as a direction worth practising, not a treatment.

## A 3-minute practice

Next time you catch yourself being harsh, try three sentences in order (silently or in writing):

1. **Mindfulness**: "This is a hard moment."
2. **Common humanity**: "Making mistakes and hurting is something everyone goes through. I'm not the only one."
3. **Self-kindness**: "I can be a little gentler with myself. What would I say to a friend in this situation?" — then say that sentence to yourself.

## Boundaries

If self-critical thoughts are intense and persistent, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, seek help right away — contact local emergency services (in the US, call or text 988).
